# Communication Benchmark

## Objective

The benchmark evaluates communication behavior for large image messages.

Two configurations are compared:

### Fast DDS

```text
Publisher
    ↓
Fast DDS
    ↓
UDP
    ↓
Subscriber
```

### CycloneDDS / Iceoryx

```text
Publisher
    ↓
CycloneDDS
    ↓
Iceoryx shared memory
    ↓
Subscriber
```

### Workload

The primary test used:

1280 × 720 video;
30 FPS;
H.264 encoding;
approximately 400 MB;
ten-minute duration.


Additional workloads were used to investigate system freezing under real-time scheduling.

### Metrics

#### Transport latency

Calculated from the ROS timestamp:

```text
arrival_time - message_timestamp
```

#### Frame drops

Frame identifiers are generated by the publisher.

The subscriber calculates:

```text
current_frame_id - previous_frame_id - 1
```

A positive result indicates frames that were not received by the subscriber.

#### Inference latency

YOLO inference time is measured separately.

This prevents AI processing time from being interpreted as middleware transport latency.

### Queueing

Both publisher and subscriber use a queue depth of one.

This intentionally prioritizes the latest frame.

For real-time perception, continuously processing old frames can be more harmful than dropping obsolete frames because it increases information age.

### Results and Interpretation

The experiment conducted three UDP/FastDDS runs and three CycloneDDS plus iceoryx shared-memory runs. The number of network drops is inferred from frame-number gaps at the subscriber. It therefore captures all
reasons why the subscriber did not process a frame, including intentional queue overwriting, inference time, callback contention, and transport behavior. Hence, the benchmark conclusion focuses especially on latency distribution and tail reduction,
while describing drop rate as a system-level symptom of overload.

The aggregate mean latency improves from 18.02 ms in the UDP/FastDDS group to 16.71 ms in the CycloneDDS plus iceoryx group. The median changes only slightly, from 12.92 ms to 12.61 ms. This means the central tendency is similar, and the practical benefit is not primarily a lower median sample. The more important improvement
is the reduction in variability and high-percentile latency.

The standard deviation decreases from 24.83 ms to 18.85 ms, and the p99 latency decreases from 103.78 ms to 41.27 ms. The maximum latency is also lower in the shared-memory group, decreasing from 544.68 ms to 301.67 ms. These values support the conclusion that shared memory improves tail behavior, which is more relevant
to safety timing than average latency. A drone safety reflex is affected by rare late frames because the vehicle continues moving during those delays.

The drop-rate values are high in both modes, 83.50 percent for UDP/FastDDS and 81.83 percent for CycloneDDS plus iceoryx. These values should be read as subscriber-level frame gaps rather than as pure network losses. The benchmark intentionally uses a YOLO subscriber and a one-frame queue, so frames are overwritten when
inference or callbacks cannot keep up. The result therefore reveals that transport optimization alone cannot compensate for an overloaded consumer.

The freezing observed when FIFO priority and core pinning were applied together is an important negative result. It shows that real-time privileges can degrade system behavior when given to unsuitable workloads. The final scheduling design is therefore
evidence-driven. FIFO is reserved for the sensor ingress path, RR is used for lowercriticality logic, and UI work is isolated.

The results are specific to the tested:

Raspberry Pi 5;
operating system;
kernel;
ROS 2 version;
DDS versions;
CycloneDDS configuration;
Iceoryx configuration;
CPU scheduling;
workload.

The results should therefore be interpreted as an experimental comparison rather than a universal performance ranking of DDS implementations.
